What does a scientific conference poster look like?

This tool builds and formats your research poster for you. You do not need to design a poster from scratch or recreate any example you see. The examples below are simply to help you recognize how scientific posters communicate research: short sections of writing, clear headings, readable figures, and concise explanations rather than long paragraphs. Focus on selecting accurate evidence and explaining it clearly; the builder will organize your work into a poster layout.

Build your research poster

Use short, accurate explanations drawn from your existing work. Cite claims using author–year references.

Suggested length: 6–14 words. Be creative but accurate. Try a question (“Does Timing Matter?”), a comparison (“Light Exposure and Seedling Growth”), or an evidence-focused title (“What Studies Suggest About Urban Tree Survival”). Avoid promising outcomes the evidence does not establish.
Suggested length: 20–45 words. State your final question. Example (hypothetical): “Among greenhouse-grown seedlings, how does daily light exposure compared with a shorter exposure affect plant height over six weeks?”
Suggested length: 40–75 words. Why does this issue matter? Give only essential context. Example: “Light exposure influences plant development, but growth patterns may differ across species and growing conditions. Comparing studies requires attention to how light and growth were measured.”

Visual 2 · Create your own evidence visualization

Your job is to turn verified research evidence into a visual that helps someone understand your findings. You are not expected to be a graphic designer or invent new data.

Start with the evidence, not the chart

  1. Find a result worth showing. Open a primary article or your Evidence Matrix. Look in the Results section or a numbered table for an actual comparison, trend, or key finding.
  2. Choose the right format. Use a bar chart for numerical group comparisons, a line graph for measurements across ordered time points, or an evidence-summary table when studies report different outcomes that should not be plotted on one numerical axis.
  3. Copy the information carefully. Keep the exact group names, numbers, measurement units, and study conditions. If the article reports change scores, do not relabel them as final scores.
  4. Explain the meaning. Give your visual a descriptive title, cite the source and exact results location, and write 2–3 sentences about what a reader should notice and what the evidence cannot establish.

Suggested caption length: 35–65 words. A clear visual usually needs only 2–6 rows or points. More is not automatically better.

Worked examples (hypothetical—not actual article results)

Hypothetical seedling height in centimeters. Use only when both groups report the same outcome and units. Do not claim a statistically significant difference unless the study supports it.

Line graph: Show change over time

Question: How did a measured outcome change across sessions?

Hypothetical repeated measurements. Use a line graph only when the x-axis has a meaningful order, such as time.

Evidence table: Compare studies

Question: What do different studies suggest when their outcomes are not directly comparable?

StudyVerified finding
Study AImprovement reported in outcome X
Study BNo clear difference in outcome Y

Hypothetical wording. Include author–year study identifiers and accurately qualified findings from the original articles.

Find it fast in your article

Look under Results, then check Table 1, Table 2, or Figures for measured outcomes. Read the table footnotes, figure legend, and Methods section to confirm units, group definitions, and time points. Use your Evidence Matrix to locate candidate findings, then verify them in the original article.

Build your visual from verified evidence

Enter 2–6 labeled values from one study. For a summary table, enter study labels and short verified findings instead of numbers.

Do not combine incompatible outcomes, invent values, or add error bars or significance claims not supported by the original study. A summary table is appropriate when graphing would mislead.

Individual research conversation · About 15 minutes

This is a conversation about your research, not a memorized presentation. Meetings may be held in person or virtually. You may refer to your poster, reflection, and notes as you explain your reasoning.

Walk through your poster

  1. What question did you investigate, and why does it matter?
  2. What do your studies suggest together?
  3. What does each of your two visuals show, and why did you choose it?

Explain the evidence

  1. Which specific finding most influenced your conclusion?
  2. Where do the studies agree, differ, or leave uncertainty?
  3. What can you reasonably conclude or recommend—and what would go too far?

Reflect on your process

  1. Which prior assignment helped change or sharpen your understanding?
  2. What is one decision you made after checking evidence or feedback?
  3. What do you understand about research now that you did not at the beginning of EXS290?

You will not necessarily be asked every question. You may use notes; confidence and public-speaking polish are not grading criteria.
